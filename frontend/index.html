Why CityLab exists

The gap: a property owner can't tell whether a roof is worth a solar call without paying for a site visit, and a researcher can't test a hunch about a city — "do greener buildings rent for more?" — without weeks of manual data work. The data to answer both questions already exists; it's just scattered across a real-estate database, satellite photos, and public weather records.

What CityLab does: ask a plain-English question about Houston or Los Angeles apartment buildings. CityLab samples a real cohort from CoStar, measures each roof from aerial imagery and sun geometry, runs the actual statistics, and writes a brief that cites exactly where every number came from. For solar questions, it ranks real buildings by payback and drafts (never sends) an email to the owner.

What it refuses to do: invent a number no tool produced, call a correlation a cause, or let a model guess at something a formula can already answer — roof area, building height, and sun exposure are arithmetic over real data, not a model's reading of a photo. The one thing it asks a vision model to do is the one thing a formula can't: count what's actually in a photograph.
